🇪🇸 Fertility treatment in Spain

Spain regulates assisted reproduction through a national law (Law 14/2006). It allows treatment for single women and female couples, requires egg and sperm donation to be anonymous and unpaid beyond compensation, and treats surrogacy contracts as legally void. Spanish centers report to a national registry run with the Ministry of Health.

By FertilityList Editorial (AI-assisted) · Sources checked 2026-11-13

This page summarizes laws and published data for general information. It is not legal or medical advice, and laws change. Confirm current rules with the clinic and an independent local lawyer.

Key facts

Who can be treated
Any woman over 18 with full legal capacity, regardless of marital status or sexual orientation[1]
Donor anonymity
Donation is anonymous; children and recipients may receive general, non-identifying donor information[1]
Donor payment
Donation must be non-commercial; only compensation for inconvenience, travel and work expenses is allowed[1]
Embryos per transfer
Maximum of three per woman per cycle[1]
Children per donor
No more than six children born in Spain from the same donor[1]
Surrogacy
Surrogacy contracts are null and void; legal maternity is determined by birth[1]

The legal framework

Access

Law 14/2006 lets any woman over 18 with full legal capacity use assisted reproduction with her written consent, independently of her marital status and sexual orientation. If she is married, her spouse’s consent is also required unless they are legally or de facto separated. Clinics must inform her of the risks of pregnancy at a clinically inadvisable age, but the law sets no fixed upper age limit.[1]

Egg and sperm donation

Donation is a free, formal and confidential contract between donor and center. Donors must be over 18 and pass a mandatory screening protocol. Donation is anonymous: recipients and children born can obtain general information about the donor, but not identity, except in exceptional circumstances such as a real danger to the child’s life or health. The medical team, not the recipient, selects the donor and seeks the greatest phenotypic and immunological similarity.[1]

Surrogacy

A contract for gestation by a woman who renounces maternity in favor of another person is null and void by law, with or without payment. The parentage of children born this way is determined by birth.[1]

Egg freezing and embryo freezing

Egg freezing for fertility preservation is growing in Spain: according to the national registry, 65,805 eggs were vitrified for that purpose in 2023, up from 58,248 in 2022, and 1,284 embryo transfers used eggs from patients who had preserved fertility. The same release reports that 212 trans patients preserved fertility in 2023. Under Law 14/2006 the donation rules above also apply to surplus eggs or embryos donated to other people.[2][1]

For how egg and embryo freezing work and how common they are in the US, see our guides on egg freezing and frozen vs. fresh embryo transfer.

Questions to ask a clinic in Spain

  • Does the clinic report its results to the national or regional registry, and can it share its own outcomes by age?
  • Which of the rules on this page apply to you as a foreign patient, and what documents will you need?
  • If you use donor eggs, sperm or embryos, what information about the donor will you and a future child be able to access?
  • How long can frozen eggs or embryos be stored, what does storage cost each year, and can they be shipped to another country?
  • How many visits are needed in person, and which steps can be done with a clinic at home?
