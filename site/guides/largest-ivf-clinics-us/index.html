The Largest IVF Clinics in the US by Cycle Volume

Which US fertility clinics perform the most IVF cycles, how concentrated treatment is, and what clinic size does and does not tell you, from CDC clinic data.

By FertilityList Editorial (AI-assisted, data-driven) · Last updated November 18, 2026

This guide is general information, not medical advice. Talk to a qualified clinician about your situation. Disclaimer.

The US has hundreds of IVF clinics, but a few very large practices perform a big share of all treatment. This guide ranks clinics by the number of assisted reproductive technology (ART) cycles they reported to the CDC for 2022. It shows how concentrated treatment is and explains what clinic size can and cannot tell you.

An ART cycle, in the CDC’s data, is a treatment cycle such as an IVF egg retrieval, a frozen embryo transfer, or an egg- or embryo-banking cycle.[1] Each clinic location reports separately. A network with several sites appears as several entries.

How concentrated is IVF?

The 452 clinics that reported 2022 outcomes performed 433,836 ART cycles in total. The 10 largest performed 18.6% of them, and the 25 largest performed 30.7%.[1]

The typical clinic is much smaller. The median clinic reported 514 cycles. The middle half of clinics reported between 262 and 1,121.[1]

Does a bigger clinic mean better results?

For patients under 35, the median success rate among the 25 largest clinics was 50.9%, compared with 47.8% among the other 381 clinics that reported a rate.[1] For patients over 40, the figures were 8.5% and 7.4%.[1] So in 2022 the largest clinics’ median was somewhat higher for under-35 patients. This describes one year of data. It does not show that size itself causes better or worse outcomes, and many smaller clinics report rates above the national figure.[1]

What volume can tell you:

  • Experience and routine. A clinic doing thousands of cycles a year has teams and lab processes that run constantly. That is one reason our FertilityList Score gives volume a 20% weight.
  • More stable statistics. Rates based on many cycles change less from year to year. A small clinic’s rate can swing because of a handful of patients.
  • Range of services. Large clinics usually offer the full range, such as egg freezing, donor eggs, genetic testing and gestational carrier programs. Many smaller clinics do too.[2]

What volume cannot tell you:

  • How you will be treated. Some patients prefer a smaller practice where they see the same doctor and nurses every visit.
  • Who the clinic treats. Large clinics may attract patients who travel after treatment elsewhere did not work, or older patients. Both lower reported rates. The CDC cautions that clinics treat different populations, which limits comparisons.[3]
  • Cost or wait times. The CDC data does not report prices or how soon a new patient can start.

Why some large clinics report a lot of banking

Some of the largest practices perform many egg- or embryo-banking cycles: cycles where eggs or embryos are frozen for later use rather than transferred. These count toward cycle volume. The CDC reports each clinic’s share of fertility-preservation and banking cycles. You can see it in the “Practice patterns” table on each clinic page, and in our egg freezing guide.[1]

How to compare clinics of different sizes

  1. Use your age group. Compare rates for the age row that applies to you, not the clinic’s overall total.
  2. Check the number behind the rate. Clinic pages show how many intended retrievals each rate is based on. A rate based on few cycles is less certain.
  3. Look at several years. Clinic pages show up to three years of CDC data. Consistency over time is more informative than one year.
  4. Compare locally. The state and city pages let you sort every reporting clinic near you by score, success rate or volume.

See also IVF success rates by state for the state-level picture, and the best IVF clinics in the US for a ranking by score rather than size.

Questions to ask

  • How many IVF retrievals and transfers did your clinic perform last year, and how many for patients my age?
  • Will I see the same physician throughout treatment? Who handles monitoring visits and procedures?
  • How many embryologists work in your lab, and how many cycles does the lab handle each year?
  • If your practice has several locations, which lab handles my eggs and embryos?
  • How soon could a new patient start, and what are the main costs?

Bottom line

A few dozen large clinics perform a big share of US IVF cycles, but most clinics are far smaller.[1] Size signals experience and gives more stable statistics. It is not a measure of success on its own. Compare clinics by age-specific outcomes, consistency and services, then talk to the ones that fit you.
