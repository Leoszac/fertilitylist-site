Endometriosis and Fertility: Diagnosis, Treatment and IVF Data

What endometriosis is, how it is linked to infertility, how it is diagnosed, which fertility treatments official sources list, and how often it appears in US IVF cycles.

Endometriosis is a common condition, and many people first hear about it when they have trouble getting pregnant. This guide explains what endometriosis is and how it is linked to infertility. It covers how it is diagnosed and the fertility treatments official US health sources list. It then shows how often the CDC records endometriosis as a reason for IVF.

What endometriosis is

MedlinePlus defines endometriosis as a disease in which tissue like the lining of the uterus grows in other places in the body.[4] The CDC’s IVF glossary gives examples of those places: the ovaries, fallopian tubes or abdominal cavity.[3]

It is common. The US Office on Women’s Health says researchers think at least 11% of women, or more than 6½ million women in the United States, have endometriosis.[5] It also says endometriosis “may affect more than 11% of American women between 15 and 44.”[5]

MedlinePlus lists two main symptoms: pelvic pain and infertility.[4]

The link with infertility

According to the Office on Women’s Health, researchers think endometriosis may affect as many as one in every two women with infertility.[5] That estimate comes from a research review cited by the Office.[5] The CDC’s national IVF data, below, uses a different measure.

Having endometriosis does not mean someone cannot get pregnant. Many people with the condition conceive. Others need treatment, which can range from surgery to IVF.[4][5]

How endometriosis is diagnosed

Endometriosis can be hard to confirm. MedlinePlus states that surgery is the only way to know for sure that you have endometriosis. It says the most common surgery used for diagnosis is a laparoscopy.[4] The Office on Women’s Health describes laparoscopy as a type of surgery that lets doctors look inside the pelvic area.[5]

A fertility workup also checks other possible causes, such as ovulation, the fallopian tubes and the partner’s sperm. Our guide to fertility testing describes the usual steps.

Fertility treatments official sources describe

MedlinePlus lists two treatments for infertility caused by endometriosis:[4]

  • Laparoscopy to remove endometriosis patches. The same type of surgery used for diagnosis can also remove endometriosis tissue.
  • In vitro fertilization (IVF). Eggs are retrieved, fertilized in a lab, and an embryo is transferred to the uterus.

The Office on Women’s Health notes that surgery is usually chosen for severe symptoms, when hormones are not providing relief, or for fertility problems.[5] The right sequence of surgery, other treatment and IVF depends on age, symptoms, the extent of the disease and other fertility factors. That is a decision for a licensed clinician. See IUI vs. IVF for how the two common procedures differ.

How often endometriosis appears in US IVF

The CDC reports the reasons patients used assisted reproductive technology (ART), such as IVF. More than one reason can apply to the same case, so the shares add up to more than 100%.[1] In 2022, endometriosis was listed as a reason in 6% of cases. Tubal factor was listed in 10%.[1]

The CDC share is much lower than the “one in two” estimate for women with infertility.[1][5] The two figures measure different things. The CDC share counts IVF cases where a clinic recorded endometriosis as a reason. It depends on whether the condition was diagnosed, and it includes many cycles for egg banking or other reasons unrelated to endometriosis. The research estimate refers to everyone with infertility. Neither figure tells an individual whether they have the condition. For the full list of reasons, see causes of infertility.

National IVF outcomes by age

The CDC does not publish IVF success rates for patients with endometriosis separately. The national figures below cover all patients using their own eggs.[1]

Under 35, 49.7% of intended retrievals led to a live birth, compared with 7.7% over 40.[1] These are averages across all diagnoses and do not predict any one person’s result. Our IVF success rates by age guide explains the measures.

Questions to ask a clinic

  1. Has endometriosis been confirmed in my case, and how does its extent affect my fertility options?
  2. Would surgery, IVF or another approach come first for someone in my situation, and why?
  3. If I have had surgery before, how does that affect your plan, including for my ovarian reserve?
  4. How do you coordinate with my gynecologist or surgeon?
  5. What are your success rates for my age group, and how many cycles are they based on?

Our questions to ask a fertility clinic has a longer checklist. If ovarian reserve comes up, see diminished ovarian reserve.

Bottom line

Endometriosis is common, and official US sources list infertility as one of its main symptoms.[4][5] Surgery is the only way to confirm it. MedlinePlus lists laparoscopy and IVF as treatments for related infertility.[4] In 2022, the CDC listed endometriosis as a reason in 6% of US IVF cases.[1] Discuss your options with a licensed clinician.
