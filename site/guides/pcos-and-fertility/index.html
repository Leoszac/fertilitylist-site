PCOS and Fertility: What the Data Shows About IVF

How PCOS affects fertility, how it is diagnosed, which treatments official sources list, and how often ovulatory problems appear in US IVF cycles.

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the conditions people most often look up when they have trouble getting pregnant. This guide explains what PCOS is and how it is diagnosed, and which fertility treatments official US health sources describe. It then shows what the CDC’s national IVF data says about ovulatory problems, the category that includes PCOS.

What PCOS is

MedlinePlus, from the US National Library of Medicine, describes PCOS as the name of a hormonal disorder. It is also called polyendocrine metabolic ovary syndrome (PMOS).[4] People with PCOS usually have at least two of a set of problems. These are irregular or absent ovulation, high levels of androgens (hormones such as testosterone), and growths or small follicles in the ovaries.[4][5]

PCOS is common. The CDC says it is a common cause of infertility, “affecting as many as 5 million women” in the US.[6] MedlinePlus calls it one of the most common causes of infertility.[4]

PCOS is not only a fertility issue. The CDC notes that women with PCOS often have insulin resistance, which increases the risk of type 2 diabetes.[6] That is one reason long-term follow-up with a clinician matters, whether or not someone is trying to conceive.

How PCOS is diagnosed

MedlinePlus states plainly that there is no specific test for PCOS.[4] Instead, a diagnosis is built from several findings. The NICHD, part of the National Institutes of Health, lists the features providers look for:[5]

  • Ovulation problems. Absent or irregular ovulation, which shows up as menstrual irregularities such as light or skipped periods.
  • High androgens. Raised androgen levels that do not result from other causes or conditions.
  • Ovarian findings on ultrasound. Growths of a specific size in one or both ovaries.

Because other conditions can cause similar signs, providers also rule those out. Our guide to fertility testing describes the hormone tests and ultrasound used in a typical fertility workup.

How PCOS affects fertility

The main way PCOS affects fertility is through ovulation. If the ovaries do not release an egg regularly, there are fewer chances each year to conceive. The CDC’s IVF glossary defines ovulatory dysfunction as a diagnostic category “used when a woman’s ovaries are not producing eggs normally.” The glossary says it includes PCOS.[3]

For the same reason, ovulation is the focus of most PCOS fertility treatment. MedlinePlus lists fertility treatments that “include medicines and procedures such as in vitro fertilization (IVF)” for people with PCOS who are having trouble getting pregnant.[4] It also lists lifestyle changes and medicines that manage other symptoms, such as insulin-sensitizing medicines and anti-androgens.[4] Which approach fits depends on a person’s age, other diagnoses, their partner’s fertility and their own goals. That is a decision for a licensed clinician.

How often ovulatory problems appear in US IVF

The CDC reports the reasons patients used assisted reproductive technology (ART), such as IVF. More than one reason can apply to the same case, so the shares add up to more than 100%.[1] The CDC does not report PCOS on its own. Its closest category is ovulatory dysfunction, which also includes other conditions.[3]

In 2022, ovulatory dysfunction was listed as a reason in 14% of cases.[1]

The share has been stable across the three years of final CDC data.[1][2] Fertility treatment for PCOS also includes medicines, not only IVF,[4] so these figures describe IVF patients only. They do not show how common PCOS is in the population. For the full list of reasons, see causes of infertility.

What national IVF outcomes look like by age

The CDC does not publish IVF success rates for patients with PCOS or ovulatory dysfunction separately. The national figures below cover all patients using their own eggs, whatever their diagnosis.[1]

Age is the strongest pattern in the data. Under 35, 49.7% of intended retrievals led to a live birth, compared with 7.7% over 40.[1] These averages do not predict any one person’s result. Choosing a clinic if you have PCOS

Most fertility clinics treat patients with PCOS regularly. The CDC data cannot show which clinics are best for PCOS specifically. It can help you compare clinics on age-specific outcomes, cycle volume and services. Each state page lists every reporting clinic, and our FertilityList Score explains how we combine those measures.

Questions to ask a clinic

  1. Which findings support a PCOS diagnosis in my case, and have other causes been ruled out?
  2. What treatment steps do you usually try before IVF for patients with ovulation problems, and why?
  3. How do you monitor patients with PCOS during ovarian stimulation, and how do you manage the risks you see most often?
  4. Should my insulin resistance or other metabolic health be checked before treatment?
  5. What are your success rates for my age group, and how many cycles are they based on?

Our questions to ask a fertility clinic has a longer checklist.

Bottom line

PCOS is a hormonal disorder. Official US sources call it one of the most common causes of infertility, mainly because it disrupts ovulation.[4][6] There is no single test for it.[4] Among US IVF cases in 2022, ovulatory dysfunction, the CDC category that includes PCOS, was listed as a reason in 14% of cases.[1][3] Discuss diagnosis and treatment options with a licensed clinician.
