Diminished Ovarian Reserve: What It Means for IVF

What diminished ovarian reserve means, how AMH and FSH tests are used, how common it is among US IVF cycles, and what CDC data shows about age and donor eggs.

“Diminished ovarian reserve” (DOR) is one of the most common reasons people in the US use IVF. This guide explains what the term means and how the tests behind it are used. It then shows what the CDC’s national data says about how common DOR is, how outcomes change with age, and the role of donor eggs.

What diminished ovarian reserve means

The CDC defines it plainly: the diagnosis means “the ability of the ovary to produce eggs is reduced,” for reasons that can be congenital, medical or surgical.[3] “Ovarian reserve” refers to the supply of eggs remaining in the ovaries. It declines naturally with age.[4]

Two points are often confused:

  • Quantity versus quality. Tests of ovarian reserve estimate how many eggs remain. They do not measure whether those eggs are healthy. MedlinePlus notes that the AMH test cannot tell you about the health of your eggs.[4]
  • Reserve versus fertility. A low reserve is not the same as being unable to conceive. According to MedlinePlus, an AMH result cannot predict whether someone will be able to get pregnant.[4]

How ovarian reserve is assessed

AMH (anti-Müllerian hormone)

AMH is made by the ovaries and measured with a blood test. MedlinePlus explains that higher levels mean a larger supply of eggs and lower levels a smaller one. The test is mainly used alongside other tests when making decisions about treating female infertility.[4] In IVF, AMH also helps estimate how the ovaries may respond to fertility medicines. MedlinePlus says low levels may mean higher doses are needed to get a response.[4]

FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone)

FSH triggers eggs to grow in the ovaries each cycle. An FSH test may be part of an evaluation when someone has not become pregnant after 12 months of trying. Because FSH changes through the menstrual cycle, the test may need to be done on a specific day.[5] Results are interpreted together with age, symptoms and other hormones such as LH. MedlinePlus cautions that at-home FSH urine tests cannot accurately tell whether the ovaries are making eggs.[5]

How common is DOR among US IVF cycles?

The CDC reports the reasons for using ART, and more than one reason can apply. In 2022, diminished ovarian reserve was listed as a reason in 26% of cases.[1] It is among the most frequent reasons, alongside egg or embryo banking and male factor infertility (see causes of infertility).

Because shares add up to more than 100%, they show how often each reason appears, not how many people have it.[1][2]

What the data shows about age and egg retrieval

The CDC does not publish outcomes separately for patients with DOR. But two national measures that relate to egg supply change sharply with age: how often an intended retrieval yields no eggs, and how often it leads to a live birth.[1]

Over 40, 15.2% of intended retrievals produced no eggs, compared with 4.7% under 35.[1] These are averages across all patients, not only those with DOR, and they do not predict any one person’s result. See IVF after 40 and IVF success rates by age for more detail.

Donor eggs

MedlinePlus notes that if a woman does not or cannot produce any eggs, donated eggs may be used.[6] In 2022, 400 of 451 reporting US clinics (88.7%) offered donor eggs.[1] National live-birth rates per transfer with donor eggs were:[1]

Donor-egg rates are reported per transfer, while own-egg rates above are per intended retrieval, so the two cannot be compared directly. Our donor egg success rates guide explains the difference. Using donor eggs also raises personal, legal and financial questions that are worth discussing with a clinic and, where relevant, a counselor or lawyer.

Egg freezing and DOR

Some people learn about their ovarian reserve while considering egg freezing. Reserve tests can help a clinic estimate how many eggs a stimulation cycle may produce.[4] National data shows fertility-preservation cycles are most common at younger ages, and that the share of intended retrievals with no eggs rises with age.[1] For a comparison of options, see embryo freezing vs. egg freezing.

Questions to ask a clinic

  1. Which tests did you use to assess my ovarian reserve, and what do my results suggest about my likely response to medication?
  2. How many eggs do patients with results like mine typically retrieve per cycle at your clinic?
  3. What are your success rates for my age group, and how many cycles are they based on?
  4. Do you treat many patients with diminished ovarian reserve? Which protocols do you consider, and why?
  5. If donor eggs might be an option, do you work with fresh donors, a frozen egg bank, or both, and what are the costs?

Bottom line

Diminished ovarian reserve means a reduced ability of the ovaries to produce eggs. The CDC listed it as a reason in 26% of cases in 2022.[1][3] AMH and FSH tests estimate egg supply, not egg quality, and they cannot predict on their own whether someone will become pregnant.[4][5] Discuss your results with a licensed clinician.
